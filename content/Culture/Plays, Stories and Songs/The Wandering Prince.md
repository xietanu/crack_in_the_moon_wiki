---
title: The Wandering Prince
---
_A musical in five acts. Attributed to the playwright Sorryn of Caer Aldan, though this has been disputed. Running time: approximately three hours, including an interval for between Acts III and IV. First performed approximately eighty years ago._

---
### Overview

The most widely performed play in the kingdom. It concerns Prince Edran of Valdenmarch who is captured by a orc war-leader, Kath, during [[The Karrath Raids]]. He meats Selen, Karth's daughter, and despite initially heated arguments, they fall in love. Almost all is lost when the fighting resumes as Valdenmarch's forces come to rescue Edran and Kath is killed. The play ends with Selen converting to [[the Church]] and Edran and Selen deciding to marry despite the objections of their cultures, and with peace established between the orcs and humans.

Audiences love the play for its sweeping romance with a hard-won happy ending. The capture is exciting. The arguments are electric. A leading pair with real chemistry make the audience swoon. The battle and Kath's death are devastating in the way good tragedy should be. The conversion and marriage feel earned. The final words add a poignancy that stick with the audience.

The play was also somewhat controversial when first performed for how sympathetically it presents orcish culture of the Karrath, as well as including a kiss between a member of the nobility and an orc. Prior to the play, the Karrath culture was generally perceived as being somewhat backwards and brutal, with the savagery of the Karrath Raids being most of what people knew about them. The play presents a more nuanced view of their culture, and introduced audiences to the orcish poetry cords. The controversy, however, largely served to get people talking about it and drove its popularity.

### Major Characters

- **Prince Edran of Valdenmarch** — the romantic lead. Sent north to negotiate, promptly captured, and forced to confront the gap between what he was told he would find and what he actually finds. Grows considerably between the first act and the third.
- **Selen** — the war-leader's daughter; many argue the play's most interesting character. Initially dismissive of Edran and the humans, her arguments with the captured Edran lead to a mutual respect and eventually attraction. The play ends with her conversion to the Church, with their marriage bringing and end to the conflict. This enemies-to-lovers arc is the show's engine.
- **Kath** — the war-leader, Selen's father. Politically clear-eyed, unsentimental, and is primary the voice of the play's critique of the human perspective. Initially presented as the villain, it is clear by the end of the play he is much more than this.
- **Brother Aldun** — a Church keeper traveling with Edran's diplomatic party. Written initially as broad comic relief — nervous around the orcs, the voice of ignorance — and shaded, by the fourth and fifth acts, into something deeper and authoritative as he oversees Selen's conversion.

### Synopsis

**Act I.** Prince Edran is dispatched north with a diplomatic retinue — including the anxious keeper Brother Aldun — to negotiate with the war-leader Kath, whose people have been raiding the northern settlements. They do not reach the meeting point. Kath's forces intercept them on the road and take them as prisoners in a swift capture that leaves no doubt about the disparity in competence. His message is clear: Kath will negotiate on his terms, not Valdenmarch's. Edran and his party are escorted to the Karrath encampment.

**Act II.** Edran is a prisoner, treated correctly but not warmly. Kath receives him formally once and makes his terms plain: the northern advance stops, or the envoy goes home in pieces. Edran is left to think on the offer, and Selen is asked to watch the prisoners.

The centre-piece of act II is the song "Unbound", in which Edran and Selen engage in heated argument about their cultures and ends with them turning their backs on each other - only to glance over their shoulders and their eyes meeting.

The song returns later in reprise, softer and with Edran and Selen now asking instead of arguing, with one of the most memorable moments being Selen showing Edran the Karrath poetry-chords. The act, and song, ends with an impulsive kiss, only for Selen to run away.

**Act III.** Valdenmarch's forces arrive to a rescue Edran, and attack without warning.

The battle is the play's loudest scene and its most technically demanding. Kath fights; he had no intention of surrendering the negotiation to force, and he does not now. Though he fights well, and he is losing, and he is ultimately slain.

Kath's death scene and speech is another notable moment from the play, condemning the human's aggression and lamenting for the loss his land and culture, though his speech is never finished. Edran and Selen throw themselves between the two armies.

**-- INTERVAL --**

**Act IV.** The aftermath. The Valdenmarch forces are keen to escort Edran home, but he initially refuses. Edran tries to honour Kath with a formal burial, but the humans and orcs fail to agree how to approach this. The play switches focus, away from this, to Selen, who finds a quiet place to mourn privately.

Brother Aldun, who spent the battle scenes alternating between prayer and panic, is the one who sits with her. He does not preach. He asks questions and listens to the answers. His conversations with Selen present a new side to him, of a thoughtful theologian — she pushes back on his beliefs, and he does not pretend to have answers he doesn't have. By the act's end she has asked him to explain the Church's rites. He does, carefully, without pressure.

The act ends with Brother Aldun overseeing a simple conversion ceremony where Selen sings an emotional solo. The staging tradition has Selen face south throughout, away from Mount Kami in the backdrop.

**Act V.** Reuniting. After the rites, Edran and she are reunited, and he is surprised to learn what she has done. They agree that there can be no more violence, but initially decide they cannot be together. One of the final scenes sees Edran dramatically running to see her, pushing through her retinue, before she departs. There, he declares they should marry despite objections from their cultures to bring about peace, and because they are in love.

Kath is present during the final marriage scene, and gets the final words of the play. He typically appears as a figure visible only upstage in shadow. Different productions use different final lines, and it is unclear which, if either, is the original:

> _"She is not gone. But she is lost."_

or:

> _"I named her for clear water._ _Water does not stop when it meets the sea._ _The fighting, the bloodshed, it means nothing now._ _Go well, daughter. Go where I could not."_

### Famous Lines

**Edran, Act I** _(the audition speech; almost always delivered looking toward where Selen is standing, though she hasn't been introduced yet)_

> _"I came expecting blades and howling dark._ _I find instead songs around a fire, a child peacefully at sleep.  An old man mending rope beside a horse._ _What enemy is this, that looks such like home?"_

**Selen, Act II** _(from the song, 'Unbound')_

> _"You call us wild. We say: unbound."_

**Kath, Act III** _(the death speech; the most performed, and most contested, part of the play)_

> _"We held the Breathing Land for a thousand years._ _We did not lose it — it was taken from us._ _Remember that, when they teach your children otherwise._ _Remember — "_ _(He dies.)_

**Selen, Act IV** _(after her conversion)_

> _"I do not mourn the north. I carry it._ _Whatever name I take, whatever rites,_ _the cords my father tied are tied inside me —_ _No ceremony changes what I know._ _This is not loss. This is a door I'm choosing._ _I step through freely. I step through."_

### Casting

Due to a lack of orc actors across much of Verintal, the orc parts in the play are almost always played by humans. Productions with more budget and prestige use masks that cover the eyes, nose and ears to differentiate the orcs. Smaller productions make do with stripes green face-paint applied to the cheeks and forehead to abstractly suggest orcish features.

In both traditions, Selen is typically played unmasked and with less makeup to ensure she is legible to the audience.

A handful of productions over the years have managed to recruit orcs for at least the main orc parts. While celebrated by critics, this tends to make the romance at the heart of the play more controversial and to incite some local, and vocal, opposition - though this probably contributes more ticket sales than it harms.
